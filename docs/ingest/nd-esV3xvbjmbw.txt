The Persian Gulf. Operation Epic Fury.
Somewhere off the Iranian coastline, a
triaran warship is cutting through the
water at 40 knots. On its flight deck, a
rocket booster ignites. A small
Delta-wing aircraft punches into the

Iran's air defense network. This moment,

the deck of a ship that our own Navy
tried to throw away. The Latoral combat
ship, LCS. The program the media called
Pentagon's floating garbage. The Freedom
class variant built around a propulsion
system that channels two diesel engines
and two gas turbines through a single
combining gearbox. The torque loads
exceeded what the gear teeth could
sustain. They ground themselves into
metal dust while the ship was underway.
USS Milwaukee LCS5 lost propulsion on
her maiden voyage one month after
delivery. USS Detroit LCS7 dead in the
water towed back to port. The chief of
naval operations, Admiral Mike Gilde,
called the combining gear problem the
most challenging engineering problem
that we have seen on any class of ship
since I have been in the Navy. The
Independence class variant had a
different disease. A triaran hull built
entirely from aluminum. Three hulls
joined by a wide connecting deck.
Aluminum saves weight. Weight saves
speed, but aluminum fatigues under the
relentless cyclic stress of ocean waves.
Structural cracks propagated through
high load junctions. a warship whose
skin was tearing itself apart. The Navy
decommissioned some of these ships
before they reached half their design
service life. Ships that never grew old
enough to fight. And yet, right now, in
the opening hours of the largest naval

billion dollar super carriers sit safely

Cracked hulls and failed modules — why the defects became features
But before we see what this ship did in
combat, we have to understand why it
even survived long enough to fight.
Because the answer is buried inside the
very defects that should have ended it.
Start with the Independence class. The
triaran design that cracked. three
narrow hulls connected by a wide
aluminum deck structure. The cracks were
real metal fatigue at high stress
joints, a material weakness that the
Navy spent years and millions trying to
fix. But look at what the triaran
geometry also produced. Three hulls
spread apart create a metaentric height,
the distance between the center of
gravity and the center of buoyancy,
significantly larger than a conventional
monohull. In naval architecture,
metacentric height determines how
violently a ship rolls. Think of the
difference between standing in a canoe
and standing on a catamaran. The wider
the stance between the hulls, the harder
it is to tip. Higher metacentric height
means the ship resists rolling. It stays
flat. That is irrelevant for a
traditional warship firing guns or
launching missiles. But for a platform
that needs to launch drones from its
deck in open ocean swells, it is
everything. A triaran deck is a stable
table in a moving sea. Now consider the
other failure. The LCS was designed
around swappable mission modules,
anti-ubmarine warfare, surface warfare,
mine countermeasures that could be
loaded and unloaded in port. The
modularity concept failed. The modules
were late, overbudget, and
underperforming. The Navy abandoned the
plug-and-play vision. But when you strip
the failed modules out, what remains? A
massive empty flight deck. over 11,000
square feet of unobstructed surface area
on the Independence class, roughly the
footprint of three tennis courts. That
empty deck was not designed to be a
feature. It was left behind by failure.
And failure sometimes leaves behind
exactly what you need. Open
architecture. The Navy stopped trying to
make LCS perfect. Instead, they made it
a modular node. Bolt a rocket assisted
drone launcher onto the deck. No
structural modification required. The
ship does not need to be good at
everything. It needs to float, move
fast, and open its deck to whatever
system the mission demands. The triaran
with the failed modules became the most
flexible launch platform in the fleet. A
stable deck, 11,000 square ft of empty
space, 40 plus knots. Now the question
becomes, what do you put on that deck

In December 2025, USS Santa Barbara
LCS32 was operating in the Arabian Gulf.
From her flight deck, a rocket booster
ignited. A small delta-wing aircraft
climbed into the sky. 10 ft long, 8ft
wingspan. That was the first shipboard

combat attack system. The first time the
United States Navy fired a one-way
attack drone from a warship at sea. Two
months later, that weapon went to war.
Lucas is an engineering paradox. Its
airframe is reverse engineered from
Iran's own Shahed 136, one of the most
effective one-way attack drones in
modern warfare. American engineers at
Spectre Works in Arizona studied what
made it effective. The Delta- wing
aerodynamics, the lowcost manufacturing,
the operational range, kept the physics
that worked and rebuilt everything else.
Then they flew it back to the country
that designed it. But what separates
Lucas from a copy is not cosmetics. It
is three engineering upgrades that
turned a crude weapon into a precision
system. Start with propulsion. The
original Shahed runs a crude
four-cylinder knockoff that broadcasts
its acoustic signature across the water.
You can hear it coming. Spectre Works
replaced it with a DA215 two-stroke
engine, 215 cm, 20 horsepower, fitted
with electronic fuel injection. The
DA215 operates at a fraction of that
noise. Lucas does not arrive silently,
but it arrives quietly enough that radar
detection, not sound, becomes the
primary warning. Then there is the
guidance. The original Shahed follows
pre-programmed GPS coordinates, fire and
forget. Lucas does not. It maintains a
continuous uplink through the Starlink
low Earth orbit satellite constellation.
That connection solves the radar horizon
problem, the physical limit where the
curvature of the Earth blocks line of
sight targeting. Through Starlink, a
command center can redirect Lucas
against moving targets beyond the
horizon. Human in the loop. The trigger
requires a person. The precision
requires a satellite. And finally, the
capability that no Shahed has ever
demonstrated, autonomous coordination.
Lucas carries swarming algorithms that
allow drones in a formation to talk to
each other, sharing trajectory data,
allocating targets, coordinating
approach vectors. A single Lucas is a
nuisance. 20 Lucas arriving from
multiple azimuths is a computational
crisis for any air defense system. Now
run the economics. Each Lucas costs
$35,000.
